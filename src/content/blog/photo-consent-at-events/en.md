Guests accept that an event may be photographed, but they expect to know it in advance and to be able to say no. A simple setup, decided before the day, avoids awkward moments and later complaints.

This guide describes common practice. It is not legal advice: rules differ between countries and between private and public events. In the European Union, a photo of an identifiable person is personal data under the GDPR, so check the requirements that apply to you with your legal contact.

## Before the event: tell people clearly

- **On the registration page**, state that photos and videos will be taken, what they will be used for (internal report, website, social media, press) and how long you keep them.
- **In the confirmation email**, repeat the information with a contact address for questions.
- **At the venue**, put a short sign at the entrance: "This event is photographed. Tell the welcome desk if you prefer not to appear."

Avoid vague wording such as "images may be used for any purpose". The more specific you are, the more people trust the process.

## On the day: make opting out easy

The opt-out must be visible to the photographer without the guest having to repeat it all day. Common options:

- a **lanyard or badge of a different colour**,
- a **small sticker** on the badge,
- a **list with photos** given to the photographer, for small events where everyone is registered.

Brief the photographer on the signal you chose and on the rule when someone is in the background: in doubt, they leave the picture out of the selection. Our [photographer brief template](/en/blog/event-photographer-brief) has a section for this.

## Minors and sensitive contexts

When children attend, ask for written consent from a parent or guardian and keep it with the registration record. Be equally careful with events where appearing could expose people: health, religion, politics, support groups. For these, consider photographing only the stage and the venue.

## After the event: deletion requests

Someone will eventually ask for a photo to be removed. Decide in advance:

1. **Who receives the request** (one named address, not the photographer's personal inbox).
2. **Where the photo is removed**: the shared gallery, your website, your social accounts.
3. **How fast you answer**: a short, fixed delay you can actually keep.

Keep a simple log of requests and of what you removed. It helps if the same person asks again, and it shows you handled the request seriously.

## A five-line checklist

- Information on the registration page and in the confirmation email
- Sign at the entrance
- Opt-out signal agreed with the photographer
- Written consent for minors
- One address and one owner for deletion requests
